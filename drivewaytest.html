The Driveway Test

Six things you can check on any roof from the ground, in about four minutes. No ladder. Bring your phone — most of these read better zoomed in on a screen than with the naked eye.

Check one of six

The first row is the one wind finds

The bottom row of shingles doesn't sit on the deck. It sits on a starter strip — a solid strip with its glue line at the bottom edge. That glue is the only thing holding the very edge of your roof down, and that edge is where wind gets its grip.

What to look for

Sight along the very bottom edge of the roof, the line just above the gutter. It should read as one solid, unbroken shadow line, dead straight the whole way.

If you see small evenly spaced notches interrupting it — roughly one every foot, at perfectly regular intervals — that's a 3-tab shingle cut up and used as starter. A purpose-made starter is solid. It has no notches, ever. The regular spacing is the tell: damage is random, manufacturing is not.

Not a story about crooks. Roofs were built this way for years, before dedicated starter was a stocked product, and plenty are still standing. The argument is that the right product exists now, it's a small line on an estimate, and the edge it protects is the edge that fails first.

Check two of six

The ridge tells you who did the work

The cap along the peak is its own product — thicker, pre-bent, made to fold over the ridge without cracking. A 3-tab shingle can be cut into squares and used instead. It's cheaper and it's already on the truck.

What to look for

Look at the ridge against the sky. A purpose-made cap reads as a deliberate, heavy band along the peak — visibly thicker than the courses below it, with a real shadow at each cap. Cut-up 3-tab reads thin and flat; the ridge almost disappears into the field.

If the peak looks skinnier than the roof underneath it, that's your answer. Then look at the very end of the ridge, at the gable, where you see the last cap edge-on. Two visible layers of thickness is the real thing. One thin layer isn't. A zoomed phone photo settles it in four seconds.

After a hard winter, sight along the peak for hairline cracks breaking the line. They tend to appear at even intervals — because they appear at the folds.

Check three of six

Count your exhaust types

An attic breathes by pulling cool air in low at the eaves and pushing hot air out high at the ridge. Both halves have to work. This is the check almost nobody knows to make, and it's the easiest one here.

What to look for

Name every kind of vent on the roof. A raised line along the peak is a ridge vent. Small square hoods partway down the slope are box vents. A round dome or a powered fan is a third kind. You should see one kind, not two.

Two exhaust types on the same attic short-circuit each other — the fan or the box vents start pulling air down through the ridge vent instead of up from the eaves, and the low end of the attic stops moving air at all. Then stand under the overhang and look straight up at the soffit: are there vents, and are they actually open? Hold a phone flashlight against one. If no light passes, nothing else does either.

Why it matters more than it sounds: a roof that can't breathe cooks its own shingles from underneath in summer and grows ice dams at the eaves in winter. It's invisible, it's rarely explained, and it's the thing most likely to shorten a roof that was otherwise installed well.

Check four of six

The corner where a roof meets a wall

Where a roof edge runs into a wall — over a garage, under a dormer — the water coming down that edge has to be thrown clear into the gutter. A small bent piece of metal called a kickout does it. It's the size of your hand.

What to look for

Find where a roof edge dies into a wall and look at the very bottom of that line, right where the gutter starts. There should be a small flared piece of metal turning the water outward, away from the wall.

If there's nothing there, water has been running down the inside of that wall every time it rains. This one hides for years and then shows up as rot behind the siding, so it's worth finding now. Look for a stain, a dark streak, or paint lifting on the wall directly under that corner.

Check five of six

Metal, or caulk pretending to be metal

Where a roof slope runs alongside a wall, the joint is sealed with step flashing — separate L-shaped pieces, one per course of shingles, each tucked up behind the siding and lapped over the one below like a staircase.

What to look for

Look for the staircase. Along a roof-to-wall joint you should be able to see a stepped pattern of small metal pieces going up the slope, each one overlapping the next, disappearing up behind the siding.

What you don't want: one long strip of metal laid over the top of the siding, or a bead of caulk doing the sealing. Caulk is a maintenance item pretending to be a flashing detail — it shrinks and pulls away, and when it does, the water goes behind it instead of over it.

Same test on a chimney. The metal should be cut into the mortar joint and folded down over the flashing below — a clean horizontal line stepping up with the brick courses. Metal run up the face of the brick and caulked is the shortcut version.

Check six of six

The metal at the edges

A thin strip of metal runs along the eaves and up the rakes — the sloped edge at the gable end — under the shingles and over the fascia. It's what makes water leave the roof cleanly instead of curling back onto the wood.

What to look for

Follow the sloped edge of the roof with your eye, from the gutter all the way up to the peak. You're looking for a consistent thin line of metal along the whole run, with the shingles sitting neatly over it and a small even overhang.

If the metal stops partway, or the overhang wanders in and out, that edge was finished by eye. Where the overhang comes up short, water runs behind the gutter and down the fascia board — which is why some houses have one stripe of peeling paint and nobody can work out why.
