The trailer lands on the driveway first, and then the roof comes off. This is the loudest few hours of the whole job, and it is also the fastest — a crew that took three days to schedule can strip a house before lunch.

⚠ Caution — falling cardboard This spread contains loose debris. It is paper. The real thing is heavier, and there is considerably more of it.

Everything that leaves the roof lands somewhere. We tarp the beds on the tear-off side and the ground under the eave before the first shingle moves, and the trailer sits where the fall is shortest — which is usually the driveway, and is the reason we asked you to move the cars.

What you'll hear: scraping, then a long rolling clatter as a section lets go, then boots. It stops for a moment and starts again. It is not the sound of anything going wrong.

What you'll find afterwards: nothing, if we've done it right. The nails are the part everyone forgets — a tear-off drops thousands of them, and they end up in grass, in mulch, in the gaps between paving. That's why the magnet comes out at the end of the day, and again the next morning.

Nine spreads from now, the magnet sweeps up the chips you just knocked off this page. Not a picture of them — the same ones, wherever they landed.
